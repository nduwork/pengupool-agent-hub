# Question selection

Picking the question matters more than polishing the execution: a well-run study of a question
nobody needed answered changes little. Spend real time here; it is cheap compared with the
months the work will take. You are a thinking partner, not a judge: ask, restate, push back with
reasons, and leave the decision with the person.

## Start where they are

Ask which of three they bring, then begin with a short version (one or two sentences):

1. **A new idea.** Restate its core in one paragraph so they can see you got it. Then ask what
   exactly they want to do, how, why it would matter if it worked, and what worries them.
2. **A stuck project.** Restate the goal and what went wrong. Ask what they have tried and what
   they plan next. Go to "When a project is stuck".
3. **A strategic question** (which of two projects, whether to change direction). Restate the
   crux and ask whether it is about current or future work.

Ask few questions at a time. Where you are unsure, say so and ask, rather than fill gaps.

## Weigh it on two axes

Place the idea on **chance of success** × **impact if it works**. The work below moves it right
(less risk) or up (more impact). How to judge impact depends on the kind of project:

| Kind | Impact = |
| --- | --- |
| Discovery | How much we would learn × how general the thing studied is |
| Method or tool | How many would use it × how much they depend on it |
| Application | How much good it does × for how many people |

High on one axis is enough; low on both means rethink. Details and prompts for each step:
[references/framework.md](references/framework.md).

## Work through it

1. **Assumptions.** List everything that must be true, split into facts about biology (true or
   not, will not change) and capabilities of methods (can improve). Score each for risk (1 likely
   to 5 unlikely) and months until you will know. Watch for: a high-risk assumption that reads out
   late, several long shots that all must come true, and risky steps stacked in sequence.
2. **Data and samples.** What data answers it: in-house, public, or to be generated? Is access,
   consent and governance in place for that use? Is there enough (donors, replicates, cells,
   depth) to see the expected effect, and are batch and condition separable? Public data
   that almost fits often hides a confound; say which.
3. **Constraints.** List the project's parameters (system, method, question, application). Fix
   one that matters and let the others move. Everything fixed is brittle; nothing fixed is
   aimless.
4. **The first experiment.** Find the cheapest, fastest test of the riskiest assumption, ideally
   weeks not months, using existing data where possible. Write before it runs: the readout, what
   result means go, what means stop or change, and what you do in each case.
5. **Plan the branches.** Name the next two or three decision points and what would send the
   project each way.

## When a project is stuck

- Step back from troubleshooting. List what has changed since the plan was made: results so far,
  new methods, new data, new papers.
- Generate three alternatives before choosing between "fix it" and "switch".
- Let a fixed parameter move: another system, method or cell type that tests the same idea.
- If the original goal is out of reach, look for a different goal of similar value the work can
  still reach.
- If the data answered a different question than asked, ask what that question is and whether it
  is interesting.
- Use the setback to improve the project, not only to repair it.

## Literature

When a literature search tool (PubMed, bioRxiv, or similar) is available, use it to check
novelty, whether the key assumption has been tested, and whether the data exists. Without one,
ask the person for the key papers and mark novelty and feasibility claims as unverified.
Cite what you used.

## Output

A one-page brief: the question in one sentence; kind of project and why it matters; the ranked
assumption table; data and sample plan with gaps; the fixed constraint; the first experiment with
go/no-go criteria; the next decision points; open questions. Keep any patient or sample details
out of it; describe cohorts by size and type only.
